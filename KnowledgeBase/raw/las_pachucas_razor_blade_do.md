## Overview

Created in 1993, *Las Pachucas, Razor Blade 'do* is a vibrant alkyd oil on canvas that perfectly exemplifies Carmen Lomas Garza’s dedication to capturing the nuances of Mexican American life. While many of her works focus on extended family gatherings and multi-generational rituals, this painting zeroes in on the intimate, private world of young women. It highlights the specific subculture of the *pachucas*, young Chicanas known for their bold fashion, assertive attitudes, and distinct styling. The artwork offers a glimpse into a bedroom where three women are preparing for an outing, engaging in the shared, collaborative ritual of grooming. Garza elevates this everyday domestic scene into a celebration of youth culture, female bonding, and the careful construction of personal and cultural identity. The painting is a beautiful testament to the spaces where young women find agency, express themselves, and build solidarity away from the public eye.

## What you see

The painting invites the viewer into a brightly lit, lavender-walled bedroom that serves as the backdrop for a quiet flurry of activity. At the center of the composition, a young woman in a blue dress sits at a wooden vanity mirror. Standing behind her is a friend in a yellow patterned dress, who is meticulously styling the seated woman's dark, voluminous hair into the titular "razor blade" hairdo. To the right, a third woman wearing a turquoise dress relaxes on a bright pink bed, casually reading a magazine and observing the styling process. The room is filled with intimate, telling details: a red dress hangs on the wall, pairs of heels and stacked books rest on the floor, and a black purse sits on the bed. Two cats—one black and stretching on a gray rug, another orange and resting near the bed—add to the domestic warmth. Above the vanity, a framed image of the Virgin Mary overlooks the scene, anchoring the modern youth culture within a traditional Catholic household.

## Cultural and historical context

The *pachuca* subculture emerged in the 1940s and 1950s, closely tied to the zoot suit culture of young Mexican American men (*pachucos*). Pachucas were rebellious and fiercely independent, rejecting the assimilationist pressures of mainstream American society as well as the strict, conservative gender roles expected within traditional Mexican families. They expressed their defiance through exaggerated fashion—such as dark lipstick, heavy makeup, tailored clothing, and towering, intricate hairstyles like the bouffant or the razor blade 'do. To achieve these spectacular hairstyles required significant time, skill, and mutual assistance, transforming the bedroom into a vital social hub. This space of grooming was not merely about vanity; it was a site of cultural resistance and community building among women. Garza’s depiction of this era validates the pachuca not as a delinquent—as they were often unfairly portrayed in the media—but as a creative, empowered architect of her own identity.

## Artist's intent and technique

Garza’s primary intent with this piece is to document and honor the lived experiences of Chicana women, specifically highlighting the beauty and solidarity found in their everyday rituals. By choosing to paint this scene, she actively counters the historical erasure of young women's subcultures and centers their experiences as worthy of fine art. Technically, Garza employs alkyd oil on canvas, a medium that allows her to achieve the bright, saturated colors and smooth, flat surfaces characteristic of her *monitos* style. Her meticulous attention to the environment—the specific style of the vanity, the pattern on the curtains, the exact posture of the women—demonstrates her role as a visual ethnographer. She uses a flattened perspective that eliminates deep shadows, ensuring that every element of the room is equally visible and important. This technique draws the viewer's eye across the entire canvas, encouraging a deep engagement with the narrative.

## Why this matters in the exhibition

Within the "Picturing the Familiar" exhibition, this artwork holds a crucial place as it broadens the scope of Garza’s narrative beyond childhood nostalgia and traditional family holidays. It demonstrates her engagement with adolescence, subculture, and the specific dynamics of female relationships. By showcasing the private spaces where young Chicanas constructed their identities, the painting adds a layer of complex social history to the exhibition. It connects the personal ritual of getting ready with the broader political act of claiming space and visibility as a Mexican American woman. For visitors, this piece offers a relatable, universally understood moment—friends preparing for a night out—while simultaneously educating them on the specific, resilient history of the pachucas. It stands as a powerful reminder that style, beauty, and friendship have long been essential tools for cultural survival and self-determination.
