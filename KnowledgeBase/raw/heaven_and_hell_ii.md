## Overview

Created in 1991, *Heaven and Hell II* is a compelling alkyd and oil painting on canvas that showcases a different facet of Carmen Lomas Garza’s artistic vision. While much of her portfolio is devoted to capturing the warm, familiar realities of earthly domestic life, this artwork ventures into the realm of allegory and spiritual dichotomy. The painting presents a stark, split-screen composition that visually separates a joyous, vibrant paradise from a dark, agonizing underworld. However, in Garza’s hands, these theological concepts are deeply infused with Chicano cultural markers and social commentary. By juxtaposing a lively community celebration against a scene of endless, fiery toil, Garza creates a complex narrative that speaks not only to religious beliefs but also to the earthly realities of labor, struggle, and the ultimate pursuit of liberation and joy within the Mexican American experience.

## What you see

The canvas is horizontally divided into two dramatically contrasting halves. The top section, representing heaven, is a bright, sunlit outdoor space bordered by a stucco wall with elegant arches and blooming bougainvillea. Here, a lively community gathering is underway. Couples are dancing gracefully on a pristine patio, while a band of musicians plays a piano, flute, guitar, and congas. The atmosphere is light, festive, and harmonious. In stark contrast, the bottom half of the painting plunges the viewer into a dark, subterranean hell. This fiery cavern is filled with jagged rocks and roaring flames. Within this inferno, several figures are depicted engaged in grueling, endless labor. They are shown carrying heavy sacks and tools, trudging over the burning ground. Interestingly, some of these toiling figures appear to have animalistic features, such as the heads of jaguars or frogs, adding a surreal, mythological layer to the punishment being endured.

## Cultural and historical context

The imagery of heaven and hell is a foundational element of the Catholic faith, which has deeply influenced Mexican and Mexican American culture. However, Garza’s interpretation of these realms is distinctly grounded in the lived experiences of her community. In Chicano culture, the *pachanga* or community dance—represented in the top half—is more than just a party; it is a vital space for cultural joy, connection, and release from the hardships of daily life. It represents an earthly paradise where community bonds are forged and celebrated. Conversely, the hellish scene of grueling, repetitive labor in the bottom half can be interpreted as a metaphor for the exploitative working conditions historically faced by many Mexican Americans, particularly in agricultural fields or industrial labor. The inclusion of animalistic features may also nod to indigenous Mesoamerican mythologies, blending Catholic iconography with pre-Columbian spiritual traditions.

## Artist's intent and technique

With this artwork, Garza expands her narrative scope to explore the psychological and spiritual dimensions of her culture. Her intent is not merely to depict a biblical afterlife, but to draw a powerful contrast between the joy of cultural communion and the suffering of systemic exploitation. Technically, she utilizes alkyd and oil to create a vivid, highly saturated palette that heightens the emotional impact of the split composition. The transition between the cool blues and greens of the upper realm and the intense reds and oranges of the lower realm is sharp and deliberate. Her signature *monitos* style is evident in the careful detailing of the dancers' clothing, the musicians' instruments, and the agonizing postures of the figures in the underworld. By applying her meticulous, folk-inspired aesthetic to a subject of grand allegory, she grounds the metaphysical in the familiar.

## Why this matters in the exhibition

In the context of the ASU Art Museum’s retrospective, *Heaven and Hell II* is a vital inclusion that reveals the depth and versatility of Garza’s practice. It challenges the perception that her work is solely confined to nostalgic or strictly autobiographical domestic scenes, showcasing her ability to tackle complex, allegorical themes with the same visual clarity. For the visitor, this artwork serves as a profound meditation on the duality of the Mexican American experience—acknowledging both the immense capacity for joy and cultural richness, as well as the historical realities of struggle and grueling labor. It adds a crucial layer of conceptual and social commentary to the exhibition, demonstrating how Garza uses the canvas not only to remember the past, but to analyze the moral and societal forces that shape her community's existence.
