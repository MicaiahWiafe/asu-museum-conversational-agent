## Overview

Created in 1972, *Raspas con Nieve* (Shaved Ice with Ice Cream) is an evocative early intaglio print by Carmen Lomas Garza. This artwork stands as a wonderful example of her foundational printmaking practice, capturing a quintessential and beloved memory of growing up in the heat of South Texas. The piece focuses on the simple, shared pleasure of enjoying a *raspa*—a traditional Mexican American shaved ice treat—during the sweltering days of a Texas summer. By immortalizing this everyday occurrence, Garza elevates a common childhood experience into a moment of profound cultural celebration. It demonstrates her lifelong, unwavering commitment to documenting the joyful, familiar rhythms of her community, ensuring that the informal spaces of Mexican American leisure are recorded with the dignity of fine art. 

## What you see

The artwork presents a lively, deeply textured scene centered around the purchase and enjoyment of *raspas*. Given the intaglio printmaking process, the image is rendered entirely through fine, incised lines and cross-hatching, creating a rich interplay of light, shadow, and depth in monochrome. The viewer is shown a scene with figures interacting around a local raspa stand or counter, which serves as the bustling focal point of the composition. The environment is depicted with Garza's characteristic attention to the specific architectural and environmental details of her childhood surroundings, though it is presented with the slightly more stylized, graphic quality that typifies her early 1970s printwork. The expressions and relaxed postures of the figures convey a distinct sense of casual community gathering, capturing the immediate, cooling relief provided by the shaved ice on a hot day. The meticulous linework invites the viewer to look closely, exploring the dense visual information packed into the modest dimensions of the print.

## Cultural and historical context

In the intense, unrelenting heat of South Texas, the local *raspa* stand operates as far more than just a place to buy a quick snack; it functions as a vital, informal community hub. Similar to a neighborhood diner or a corner store, it is a safe, accessible space where neighbors cross paths, children gather after school or on long weekend afternoons, and essential social bonding occurs. The *raspa* itself—finely shaved ice generously drenched in vibrant, intensely flavored syrups like tamarindo, chamoy, or strawberry, and sometimes topped with a rich scoop of ice cream (*nieve*)—is a deeply nostalgic culinary staple for Mexican Americans. During the early years of the Chicano civil rights movement, when Garza was actively creating this piece, there was a concerted, highly political effort among artists to find beauty and validity in these exact types of hyper-local, working-class community spaces. These were the environments entirely overlooked or dismissed by mainstream American culture, making their depiction a quiet act of cultural assertion.

## Artist's intent and technique

Garza's primary intent with *Raspas con Nieve* is to validate and preserve the specific, joyful cultural memories of her youth. By deliberately choosing the intaglio printmaking technique—which involves painstakingly incising an image into a metal plate, forcing ink into the grooves, and running it through a high-pressure press onto paper—she commits this fleeting summer memory to a permanent, respected fine art medium. Printmaking was a particularly important practice for Garza and her peers in the early 1970s because of its democratic nature; it allowed for multiple original copies of an artwork to be produced and widely shared within the working-class community. Her masterful use of line work in this piece is both descriptive and atmospheric, effectively capturing not just the physical appearance of the scene, but the nostalgic, hazy, and comforting feeling of a childhood summer day in Kingsville.

## Why this matters in the exhibition

Within the comprehensive "Picturing the Familiar" exhibition at the ASU Art Museum, *Raspas con Nieve* serves as a crucial anchor to Garza's early career and her deep roots in the printmaking tradition. It proves to visitors that her dedication to documenting Chicano life was firmly established from the very beginning of her artistic journey, long before she gained national fame for her larger, full-color gouache and oil paintings. For the museum visitor, this artwork offers a highly accessible and relatable entry point—the universal, cross-cultural childhood joy of a cold treat on a hot day—while simultaneously providing a specific, culturally grounded look at community life in South Texas. It reminds viewers that significant, enduring cultural history is often found in the most informal, joyous, and seemingly ordinary moments of our everyday lives.
