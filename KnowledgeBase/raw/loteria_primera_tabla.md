## Overview

Created in 1972, *Lotería—Primera Tabla* (Lottery—First Board) is an early and highly significant etching by Carmen Lomas Garza. This artwork demonstrates her foundational roots in printmaking and her early engagement with the visual vocabulary of the Chicano civil rights movement. The piece takes its inspiration directly from the traditional Mexican game of *lotería*, a game of chance similar to bingo, but played with a deck of vividly illustrated cards instead of numbered balls. By adopting the familiar grid format of the *lotería* board (*tabla*), Garza elevates a commonplace object of leisure into a sophisticated piece of fine art. In doing so, she taps into a universally recognized cultural touchstone, using it as a framework to explore themes of identity, memory, and the shared visual language that binds the Mexican American community together across generations.

## What you see

The artwork presents a rectangular grid divided into multiple small, equal-sized cells, exactly mimicking the layout of a playing board used in the game of *lotería*. However, instead of the commercially produced, mass-market images typically found on these boards (such as *El Gallo* or *La Sirena*), Garza has intricately etched her own personalized iconography into each square. The cells contain a mix of traditional symbols, references to South Texas life, and deeply personal motifs drawn from her childhood memories and cultural heritage. You might see precise renderings of local flora, common household objects, or figures engaged in everyday activities. Because it is an etching, the artwork is monochromatic, relying on the stark, elegant contrast of rich black ink against the paper. The fine lines and varied shading techniques showcase Garza’s meticulous draftsmanship and her ability to pack dense narrative meaning into very small, confined spaces.

## Cultural and historical context

*Lotería* is far more than just a game in Mexican and Mexican American culture; it is an informal educational tool and a repository of collective visual memory. Played at kitchen tables, church fairs, and community centers, it is often one of the first ways children learn to associate Spanish words with specific images. During the 1970s, as the Chicano movement fought for bilingual education and cultural representation, artists frequently turned to familiar, popular forms like *lotería* to communicate their messages. These recognizable formats bypassed the elitism of traditional European art history, speaking directly to the working-class community in a language they already owned. Printmaking, too, was a highly favored medium during the movement because it allowed for the democratic, widespread distribution of images. *Lotería—Primera Tabla* reflects this era’s desire to look inward, finding profound artistic value in the everyday objects of the home.

## Artist's intent and technique

Garza’s intent with this etching is to validate the cultural artifacts of her youth, proving that the aesthetics of a working-class Mexican American household are worthy subjects for fine art printmaking. The choice of the *lotería* grid allows her to present a fragmented, nonlinear autobiography—a collection of visual memories that together form a complete identity. The etching process she employed is labor-intensive and unforgiving. It involves using a sharp needle to scratch an image into a protective ground on a metal plate, which is then submerged in acid. The acid bites into the exposed metal, creating the grooves that will hold the ink. Garza’s mastery of this technique is evident in the crisp, confident lines and the subtle textures she achieves within each tiny square of the board, requiring the viewer to step very close to fully appreciate the detail.

## Why this matters in the exhibition

Within "Picturing the Familiar," *Lotería—Primera Tabla* serves as a crucial early benchmark in Garza’s career. It shows visitors how her artistic philosophy was formed during her university years and her involvement in the Chicano movement. The artwork highlights her early realization that true cultural representation didn't necessarily require grand, sweeping political murals; it could be achieved by carefully, lovingly elevating the simple, everyday objects that define community life. For the museum visitor, the piece acts as a visual puzzle and a nostalgic touchstone. It demonstrates that the impulse to categorize, remember, and celebrate one's culture can take the playful, familiar form of a beloved childhood game.
