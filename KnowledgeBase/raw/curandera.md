## Overview

Painted in 1977, *Curandera* is a defining gouache on cotton paper artwork that beautifully illustrates Carmen Lomas Garza’s commitment to documenting the spiritual and medicinal traditions of her community. This intimate painting captures a crucial aspect of Mexican American life that is often hidden from the mainstream public: the practice of *curanderismo*, or traditional folk healing. The artwork provides a respectful, dignified view into a domestic bedroom where a healer is actively performing a remedy on a sick individual, surrounded by watchful family members. By bringing this deeply private, faith-based practice into the realm of fine art, Garza not only validates the holistic knowledge of her ancestors but also highlights the self-reliance and communal care that sustained working-class Mexican American communities when formal medical care was either inaccessible or culturally alienating. 

## What you see

The artwork invites you into a warmly lit, modest bedroom that has been temporarily transformed into a space of healing. In the center of the room, a woman lies in a bed, appearing ill or distressed. Standing over her is the *curandera*, an older female healer, who is actively engaged in a spiritual and physical cleansing ritual, possibly a *barrida* (a sweeping). The healer holds traditional implements—often a bundle of specific herbs like rue or sage, or an egg—used to draw out illness or negative energy. The room is heavily populated by the sick woman’s family members, who stand near the walls and doorways, watching the process with expressions of deep concern, respect, and faith. The domestic setting is rich with cultural markers: a framed picture of the Virgin of Guadalupe hangs on the wall, serving as a spiritual anchor for the healing process, and the patterned linoleum floor and simple wooden furniture ground the mystical event in a very recognizable, everyday working-class home.

## Cultural and historical context

*Curanderismo* is a complex, holistic healing system that blends indigenous Mesoamerican medicinal knowledge, Spanish Catholic iconography, and traditional herbalism. For generations of Mexican Americans living in the Southwest, particularly in rural or segregated areas like the South Texas of Garza's youth, the *curandera* or *curandero* was a vital community figure. Due to systemic poverty, language barriers, and a history of discrimination within formal healthcare systems, families frequently relied on these traditional healers to address both physical ailments and spiritual or psychological distress, such as *susto* (fright or soul loss). The practice is deeply rooted in community trust and faith. Historically, mainstream society often dismissed these practices as mere superstition or ignorance, making Garza’s reverent, serious depiction of the *curandera* a profound act of cultural defense and validation.

## Artist's intent and technique

Garza’s primary intent with this painting is to counter the negative stereotypes and misunderstandings surrounding traditional Mexican American healing. She aims to present the *curandera* not as a caricature, but as a skilled, respected practitioner of community care. Technically, the use of gouache allows Garza to build flat, opaque layers of color, resulting in a vibrant, highly readable composition. Her meticulous attention to the environment—the placement of the religious icons, the specific posture of the healer, the varied ages of the family members present—demonstrates her approach as a visual ethnographer. By utilizing a flattened perspective, she ensures that the viewer takes in the entire context of the room, emphasizing that the healing process is a collective, communal event rather than just an isolated interaction between a doctor and a patient.

## Why this matters in the exhibition

In the context of the ASU Art Museum’s retrospective, *Curandera* is a cornerstone piece that speaks to the exhibition's themes of resilience and alternative systems of knowledge. It invites the visitor to broaden their understanding of what constitutes medicine and care. For many visitors of Mexican American descent, the painting evokes deeply personal, often unspoken family histories, offering a moment of powerful recognition within a formal museum space. For those unfamiliar with the culture, it serves as a warm, respectful introduction to a foundational aspect of Chicano spiritual life. The artwork underscores Garza’s overarching belief that the domestic sphere is not just a place of rest, but a site of profound cultural power, physical healing, and spiritual endurance.
