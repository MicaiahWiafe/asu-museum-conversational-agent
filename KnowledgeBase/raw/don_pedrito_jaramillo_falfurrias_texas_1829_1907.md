## Overview

Created in 1976, *Don Pedrito Jaramillo, Falfurrias, Texas 1829-1907* is a remarkable copper plate etching with paint that highlights Carmen Lomas Garza’s deep connection to the spiritual history of her native South Texas. The artwork is structured as a triptych—a three-paneled format historically associated with religious altarpieces. It serves as a visual monument to one of the most revered historical figures in the Texas-Mexico borderlands: Don Pedrito Jaramillo, a legendary *curandero* (folk healer) known affectionately as the "Healer of Los Olmos." By dedicating this complex, multi-paneled print to his memory, Garza bridges the gap between historical documentation and spiritual veneration, honoring a figure whose legacy of compassion and healing continues to profoundly influence the cultural landscape of the Mexican American community.

## What you see

The artwork is presented in a traditional triptych format, consisting of a large central panel flanked by two slightly smaller side panels, creating a physical sense of an opened altar or shrine. In the dominant central panel, the figure of Don Pedrito Jaramillo is prominently featured. He is depicted as an older, dignified man with a long, flowing white beard, seated in a calm, authoritative posture, reflecting the historical photographs that exist of him. The surrounding space in the central panel and the flanking side panels are filled with intricate imagery related to his life and healing practice. You see depictions of the natural landscape of South Texas, the humble structures where he received the sick, and symbols representing the ailments he cured and the faith of the thousands who traveled to seek his help. The underlying image is created through the precise, delicate lines of copper plate etching, while the strategic application of paint adds selective areas of color, drawing the eye to key elements of the narrative and giving the piece the aura of a sacred, illuminated manuscript.

## Cultural and historical context

Don Pedrito Jaramillo (1829–1907) is a historical giant in the Rio Grande Valley and South Texas. Born in Mexico, he settled near Falfurrias, Texas, where he claimed to have received a gift of healing from God after recovering from a severe injury. For over two decades, he treated thousands of patients—Mexican Americans, Anglo settlers, and indigenous people alike—never charging a set fee and prescribing simple remedies that relied heavily on faith, water, and basic herbs. In an era marked by intense racial violence, segregation, and extreme poverty in South Texas, Don Pedrito provided critical care to a marginalized population that had virtually no access to professional medical doctors. Following his death, he was elevated to the status of a folk saint. His shrine in Falfurrias remains a major pilgrimage site today. By creating this artwork, Garza taps into a deeply rooted, localized spirituality that exists outside the official bounds of the Catholic Church but is central to Chicano identity in the region.

## Artist's intent and technique

Garza’s intent is to assert the historical and spiritual legitimacy of Don Pedrito Jaramillo, placing him within the formal context of a gallery space. By utilizing the triptych format, she consciously adopts the visual language of European religious art, demanding that the viewer accord Don Pedrito the same reverence traditionally reserved for officially canonized saints. The technique of copper plate etching allows her to capture incredible textural detail, from the wrinkles on the healer's face to the arid brush of the Texas landscape. The addition of hand-applied paint over the etching is a delicate process that softens the starkness of the printed line, infusing the historical portrait with warmth, life, and a sense of devotional care. It demonstrates her desire to not just record history, but to lovingly embellish and honor it.

## Why this matters in the exhibition

In the ASU Art Museum’s retrospective, this artwork expands the exhibition's exploration of healing from the private, domestic sphere (as seen in *Curandera*) into the broader historical and public realm. It provides visitors with a specific, localized history lesson about South Texas, illustrating how folk heroes shape the cultural memory of a region. The piece challenges visitors to consider how communities create their own saints and support systems when neglected by the mainstream society. For those familiar with Don Pedrito, the artwork is a powerful moment of cultural validation; for others, it is a beautifully crafted introduction to a legendary figure whose legacy of radical empathy remains a cornerstone of Mexican American heritage.
