## Overview

Painted in 1980, *Abuelitos Pizcando Nopalitos* (Grandparents Harvesting Little Cactus Pads) is a tender and evocative gouache painting on cotton paper by Carmen Lomas Garza. This artwork beautifully captures a quiet, early morning ritual that is deeply embedded in the agricultural and culinary traditions of the Mexican American Southwest. The painting focuses on an elder couple engaged in the careful, skilled labor of foraging for *nopalitos*, the young, tender pads of the prickly pear cactus. Through this serene landscape scene, Garza pays homage to the enduring wisdom of the older generation, the sustainable practices inherited from indigenous ancestors, and the profound, life-sustaining connection between the Chicano community and the often-harsh natural environment of South Texas.

## What you see

The viewer is transported to a rural, arid landscape characteristic of the South Texas brush country. The scene is set in the soft, diffused light of early morning, before the intense heat of the day sets in. At the center of the composition are two older figures—the *abuelitos* (grandparents)—surrounded by large, sprawling patches of prickly pear cacti. The man and woman are dressed practically for the rugged environment, wearing long sleeves and hats to protect against the sun and the thorns. They are actively engaged in harvesting the *nopalitos*. You can see them using specific tools, likely a long knife or a specialized stick, to carefully sever the tender, bright green pads from the mature plant without getting pricked by the formidable spines. The couple works in quiet harmony, demonstrating a deep familiarity with the land. The background features more desert flora and a vast, open sky, emphasizing the isolation and the natural beauty of the foraging grounds. Garza’s use of gouache results in flat, vibrant fields of color that clearly define the sharp contours of the cacti against the softer figures of the elders.

## Cultural and historical context

The prickly pear cactus, or *nopal*, is an immensely powerful symbol in Mexican and Mexican American culture; it is famously depicted on the national flag of Mexico, rooted in the founding mythology of the Aztec empire. Beyond its symbolic weight, the *nopal* is a vital, nutritious food source that has sustained indigenous populations in the Americas for millennia. The knowledge of how to safely harvest and prepare it has been passed down through countless generations. In South Texas, foraging for *nopalitos* in the early spring is a cherished tradition, particularly among the older generations who maintain a close relationship with the land. This practice is not merely about securing free food; it is an act of cultural continuity, a way of maintaining dietary traditions that tie the community to their ancestral past. By depicting the *abuelitos* performing this task, Garza highlights the crucial role that elders play as the active keepers and transmitters of this environmental and cultural knowledge.

## Artist's intent and technique

Garza’s intent with this painting is to express profound gratitude and respect for the labor and resilience of the generations that came before her. She aims to elevate a humble, rural chore into a subject of artistic reverence. Technically, the gouache medium allows her to render the harsh, thorny landscape with an unexpected softness and warmth. Her meticulous attention to the specific posture of the figures and the accurate depiction of the cactus plants reflects her role as a visual storyteller who prioritizes authenticity. By focusing on the quiet cooperation between the couple, she paints a portrait of enduring love—not just romantic love, but a shared devotion to survival, tradition, and the land itself. 

## Why this matters in the exhibition

Within "Picturing the Familiar," *Abuelitos Pizcando Nopalitos* broadens the exhibition's scope from the interior spaces of homes and backyards out into the wider natural landscape. It demonstrates to the museum visitor that Chicano culture is deeply tied to the specific ecology of the Southwest. The artwork serves as a poignant reminder of the dignity of manual labor and the importance of ecological knowledge. For visitors, it offers a moment of reflection on the sources of their own food and the ancestral knowledge required to harvest it. It perfectly encapsulates Garza’s overarching narrative: that true cultural wealth lies in the everyday practices, shared knowledge, and enduring love of the family unit.
